# The Mechanics of ESG Investing: A Technical Guide

## Overview
A comprehensive technical guide to ESG investing frameworks, regulations, and valuation models. From MSCI ratings methodology to EU taxonomy compliance, master the data-driven discipline that evaluates risks and opportunities unaccounted for in traditional financial statements.

## 1. ESG is a Data-Driven Risk Discipline
ESG is not merely a label for "ethical" companies; it is a quantitative framework that evaluates risks and opportunities unaccounted for in traditional financial statements. It has evolved from qualitative Corporate Social Responsibility (CSR) to a rigorous, financially material discipline.
- **The Core Premise:** Environmental (e.g., carbon pricing), Social (e.g., labor strikes), and Governance (e.g., board corruption) issues inevitably translate into financial impacts. ESG metrics aim to price these externalities before they hit the P&L.

## 2. Ratings Methodologies: MSCI vs. Sustainalytics
The ESG data landscape is highly fragmented, with different providers measuring fundamentally different things.
- **MSCI (Relative / Best-in-Class):** Measures a company's resilience to ESG risks relative to its industry peers. An oil company can have an "AAA" rating if it manages its emissions and safety protocols better than other oil companies.
- **Sustainalytics (Absolute Risk):** Measures the absolute, unmanaged ESG risk magnitude. An oil company will likely receive a "High Risk" score simply because of the inherent, unavoidable risks of fossil fuel extraction.

## 3. Financial Materiality (SASB vs. GRI)
Not all ESG data is relevant to every company. Materiality frameworks determine which data points matter.
- **SASB (Financial Materiality):** Focuses on ESG issues that directly impact a company's financial condition or operating performance. For a software company, data privacy is highly material; water usage is not.
- **GRI (Impact Materiality):** Focuses on a company's external impact on the economy, environment, and people, regardless of immediate financial consequence.

## 4. The Regulatory Landscape (EU Taxonomy & SFDR)
The European Union has codified ESG into law, fundamentally changing global asset management.
- **The EU Taxonomy:** A rigorous, science-based classification system defining exactly what economic activities qualify as "environmentally sustainable." It prevents arbitrary greenwashing.
- **SFDR (Sustainable Finance Disclosure Regulation):** Forces asset managers to classify their funds:
    - **Article 6:** No sustainability scope.
    - **Article 8 ("Light Green"):** Promotes E/S characteristics.
    - **Article 9 ("Dark Green"):** Has sustainable investment as its core objective.

## 5. Integrating ESG into Valuation
ESG factors are directly integrated into quantitative financial models.
- **Cost of Capital (WACC):** High ESG risk translates to a higher cost of debt and equity. Investors demand a higher risk premium for companies facing stranded asset risks or regulatory fines.
- **Cash Flow Adjustments:** Models must adjust future cash flows for incoming carbon taxes, required CapEx for green transition, or potential litigation costs.
