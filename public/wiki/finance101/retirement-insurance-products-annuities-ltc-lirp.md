## Overview

Annuities, long-term care (LTC) insurance, and permanent life insurance are frequently marketed as investment vehicles, but they are fundamentally tools for risk transfer. Their fees are the implicit premium for insurance guarantees, which is why their investment performance is structurally designed to lag a simple S&P 500 index fund. The right question isn't "which has the best return" but "which specific catastrophic risk am I trying to offload, and can I afford not to."

## Key Concepts

- **Longevity Risk** — the risk of outliving your financial assets. Income annuities solve this directly by converting a lump sum into a guaranteed payment stream, creating a personal pension.
- **Sequence of Returns Risk** — a market downturn early in retirement drawdown can permanently impair a portfolio's ability to recover, even if long-run average returns are fine. Guaranteed-income products buffer against this.
- **LTC (Long-Term Care) Cost Risk** — standard health insurance and Medicare don't cover custodial care. A private nursing home room exceeded $116,800/year in 2023, and roughly 56% of people turning 65 will need some form of LTC.
- **LIRP (Life Insurance Retirement Plan)** — not a formal account type, but a strategy of using a permanent life insurance policy's tax-advantaged cash value (tax-deferred growth, tax-free loans/withdrawals up to basis, tax-free death benefit) as a supplemental retirement asset.

## The Cost of Guarantees

- **Variable annuities** stack multiple fee layers (M&E risk charge, admin fees, subaccount expenses, optional riders) into a total drag of roughly 3.5%/year — turning a 10% gross return into ~6.5% net. Over 20 years on a $100,000 investment, that gap can exceed $200,000 versus a low-cost index fund.
- **Fixed annuities (MYGAs)**, by contrast, were competitive with CDs and Treasuries as of 2024 — a reasonable choice for conservative, tax-deferred savings without the fee drag of variable products.
- **LTC insurance** is a probability-weighted decision, not an investment: a couple paying ~$5,000/year in premiums would need 23 years of payments just to match the cost of a single year of nursing home care. The point isn't return — it's preventing one catastrophic cost from wiping out a portfolio.
- **LIRP** value is best measured as a tax-equivalent yield: `Tax-Equivalent Yield = Tax-Free IRR / (1 - Marginal Tax Rate)`. A 4.5% tax-free IRR is equivalent to a 7.14% pre-tax return for someone in the 37% bracket — competitive, but only for money that's already maxed out 401(k)/IRA/HSA space.

## Common Pitfalls

- Confusing an annuity's "income base" (used to calculate future guaranteed withdrawals) with its actual "account value" — a "guaranteed 8% return" claim almost always refers to the former, not real investment growth.
- Underestimating illiquidity: annuity surrender charges and LIRP break-even periods (often 10+ years) make these poor vehicles for money that might be needed soon.
- Ignoring inflation: a fixed annuity payout loses purchasing power over time unless a (costly) COLA rider is added.
- LTC underwriting gets sharply harder with age — denial rates rise from ~30% at ages 60-64 to ~47% at ages 70-74, so applying in one's 50s materially improves approval odds and pricing.

## Suitability by Profile

| Net Worth | Annuity | LTC Insurance | LIRP |
|---|---|---|---|
| <$500k | Potentially suitable | Generally unsuitable (Medicaid) | Unsuitable — prioritize 401k/IRA |
| $500k-$2M, conservative | Highly recommended | Highly recommended (asset protection) | Potentially suitable (niche) |
| >$2M | Potentially suitable | Decision to self-insure | Highly recommended (estate planning) |

## Key Takeaways

- These products should be evaluated on their risk-transfer function first, investment return second — judging them purely as investments misses the point of buying them.
- An insurance guarantee is only as strong as the insurer behind it; check A.M. Best, Moody's, and S&P ratings (look for A++ or AA+) before committing.
- The "necessity" of each product scales with net worth in a U-shape: too little money and self-funding/Medicaid make sense, too much and self-insuring makes sense, and a middle band benefits most from transferring the risk.
- A LIRP's real strategic value can be as a non-correlated liquidity source during a market crash — a tax-free policy loan avoids having to sell depressed equities to raise cash.
