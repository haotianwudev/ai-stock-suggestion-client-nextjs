## Overview

By simple historical metrics, the U.S. stock market in late 2025 looks expensive: forward P/E of 22.7x versus a 10-year average of 18.6x, CAPE above 40x, and a Buffett Indicator over 200% of GDP. But absolute valuation multiples only tell part of the story — models that account for the prevailing interest rate environment paint a more nuanced, if still precarious, picture.

## Key Concepts

- **Absolute vs. relative valuation** — metrics like P/E, CAPE, and the Buffett Indicator (Market Cap-to-GDP) judge stocks in isolation. The Equity Risk Premium (ERP) and Fed Model instead judge stocks *relative to* the return available on risk-free bonds, which matters because a given P/E is more or less expensive depending on where bond yields sit.
- **Equity Risk Premium (ERP)** — the excess return investors demand for holding stocks over risk-free bonds: `Implied ERP = Earnings Yield (E/P) - 10-Year Treasury Yield`. A low ERP means investors are accepting minimal extra compensation for equity risk.
- **The Fed Model** — compares the earnings yield directly to the 10-year Treasury yield without any risk-premium adjustment. Academically flawed (it ignores that stocks and bonds carry different risk profiles), but influential as the basis for the "TINA" (There Is No Alternative) narrative that keeps capital in equities when yields are unattractive elsewhere.
- **Market concentration** — when a handful of mega-cap stocks account for a large share of an index's total weight and carry higher multiples than the rest of the index, the index-level valuation diverges from the "average stock." In this environment, the "Great 8" traded at 35.4x P/E versus 21.5x for the remaining ~492 S&P 500 companies.

## The Late-2025 Numbers

| Metric | Value | Read |
|---|---|---|
| Forward P/E (S&P 500) | 22.7x vs. 18.6x 10-yr avg | Overvalued |
| CAPE | >40x vs. ~27x 10-yr avg | Strongly overvalued |
| Buffett Indicator | >200% vs. ~150% 10-yr avg | Strongly overvalued |
| Implied ERP | ~0.31% vs. ~5.5% historical avg | Multi-decade low |

The gap between the absolute metrics (strongly overvalued) and the relative ERP reading (compressed but not obviously irrational given low real yields) is the core tension in interpreting this market.

## What's Supporting the Valuation

- **Monetary policy pivot** — two 2025 Fed rate cuts to a 3.75%-4.00% target range, despite inflation still running above the 2% target, signaling the Fed's priorities shifted toward supporting the labor market (a "Fed Put").
- **Structurally higher margins** — S&P 500 net profit margins at 12.8% are attributed to a durable compositional shift toward asset-light tech/healthcare firms, a decades-long decline in effective corporate tax rates, and lower interest expense from a long-term decline in borrowing costs.
- **Record buybacks** — announced buybacks on pace for $1.1 trillion in 2025, mechanically boosting EPS by reducing share count and providing steady demand for shares.

## What's at Risk

- A compressed ERP near multi-decade lows offers little cushion if bond yields rise or growth disappoints.
- Persistent inflation (3% headline/core) limits how much further the Fed can ease, and any reversal to a hawkish stance would remove a key support.
- The market is priced for a "soft landing" — not for a recession (which would hit earnings) or an inflation resurgence (which would force hawkish policy).
- Concentration risk: index-level health depends disproportionately on a small number of mega-cap names.

## Key Takeaways

- A high P/E alone doesn't tell you whether a market is "too expensive" — the ERP reframes the same data relative to the opportunity cost of holding bonds instead.
- "Justified but precarious" can both be true at once: current valuations can be a rational response to today's specific rate/inflation/earnings mix while still leaving very little margin for error if any of those conditions shift.
- Index-level valuation metrics can mask a large divergence between mega-cap leaders and the broader constituent set — check both.
- The Fed Model's popularity as a market narrative (TINA) doesn't make it academically sound; it ignores the different risk characteristics of stocks and bonds.
